From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Not to be confused with [Romanization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanization "Romanization"), the conversion of text written in another script into Latin (or Roman) letters, or [Liturgical Latinisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liturgical_Latinisation "Liturgical Latinisation").

**Latinisation** (or **Latinization**)[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names#cite_note-latinisation-def-1)**of names**, also known as **onomastic Latinisation**, is the practice of changing certain non-[Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") names into a form that more closely fits the [style, structure, and rules of Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Latin "Neo-Latin").[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names#cite_note-latinisation-def-1) The practice is found with historical [proper names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_names "Proper names"), including [personal names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_name "Personal name") and [toponyms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toponym "Toponym"), and in the standard [binomial nomenclature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_nomenclature "Binomial nomenclature") of the life sciences. In present-day English, the practice of using Latinised names for certain prominent individuals began in the [medieval period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_period "Medieval period") of Europe and peaked in the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance "Renaissance"). In some cases, the individuals themselves even used or created these Latinised names.

Latinisation is distinct from [romanisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanisation "Romanisation"), which is the [transliteration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transliteration "Transliteration") of a word to the [Latin alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabet "Latin alphabet"), more or less letter-for-letter or sound-for-sound, from another script (e.g. the writing systems of [Cyrillic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrillic "Cyrillic"), [Devanagari/Hindi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari "Devanagari"), [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script "Arabic script"), etc.). For authors writing in Latin, this change allows the name to function grammatically in a sentence through [declension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declension "Declension"). Many other reasons for an author to apply this to their own name also existed, such as forming a more international identity or hiding the modest social background revealed by their origin name.

In a scientific context, the main purpose of Latinisation may be to produce a name which is internationally consistent.

Latinisation may be carried out by:

*   transforming the name into Latin sounds (e.g. _[Geber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geber\_(disambiguation) "Geber (disambiguation)")_ for _[Jabir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jabir\_(name) "Jabir (name)")_), or
*   adding Latinate suffixes to the end of a name (e.g. _Meibomius_ for _[Meibom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich\_Meibom\_(doctor) "Heinrich Meibom (doctor)")),_ or
*   translating a name with a specific meaning into Latin (e.g. _Venator_ for Italian _Cacciatore_; both mean 'hunter'), or
*   choosing a new name based on some attribute of the person (e.g. [Daniel Santbech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Santbech "Daniel Santbech") became _Noviomagus_, possibly from the Latin (actually Latinised [Gaulish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaulish "Gaulish")) name for the town of [Nijmegen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nijmegen "Nijmegen"), and meaning 'new field').

[![Image 1](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/09/De_Verborum_frontispiece.jpg/250px-De_Verborum_frontispiece.jpg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:De_Verborum_frontispiece.jpg)

[Frontispiece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_frontispiece "Book frontispiece") of a 1743 legal text by [Barnabé Brisson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnab%C3%A9_Brisson "Barnabé Brisson") shows his name Latinised in the genitive _Barnabae Brissonii_ ('of _Barnabas Brissonius_'). Barnabas is itself a Greek version of an Aramaic name.

English, and sometimes other modern European languages, use Latinised names for certain historical figures from the medieval and particularly Renaissance periods, or in references that begin in the Renaissance to even older figures.

[Renaissance humanists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_humanist "Renaissance humanist") largely assumed Latinised names, though in some cases (e.g. [Melanchthon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanchthon "Melanchthon")) they invoked [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek "Ancient Greek"). For instance, [Beatus Bild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beatus_Bild "Beatus Bild") from [Rhinau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhinau "Rhinau") assumed the Latinised named Beatus Rhenanus. Latinisation in humanist names may consist of translation from vernacular European languages, sometimes involving a playful element of [punning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pun "Pun"). Such names could also be a cover for humble social origins.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names#cite_note-2)

The title of the "[Wilhelmus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelmus "Wilhelmus")", [national anthem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_anthem "National anthem") of the [Netherlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_the_Netherlands "Kingdom of the Netherlands"), preserves a Latinised form of the name of [William the Silent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Silent "William the Silent").[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names#cite_note-3)

Some English examples of Latinised names that are typical for certain historical figures include:

*   The ancient Chinese philosopher [Confucius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucius "Confucius"), from his Chinese name _Kong Fuzi_ (lit.'Master Kong')
*   The ancient Chinese philosopher [Mencius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mencius "Mencius"), from his Chinese name _Mengzi_ (lit.'Master Meng')
*   The [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age "Islamic Golden Age") philosopher and medical scholar [Avicenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avicenna "Avicenna"), from his Arabic name _ibn Sina_ (lit.'male descendant of Sina')
*   The German Christian scholar and saint [Albertus Magnus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albertus_Magnus "Albertus Magnus"), meaning _Albert the Great_, from the original German _Albert_
*   The Italian Christian scholar and saint [Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas "Thomas Aquinas"), originally the Italian _Tommaso_ plus his hometown of _[Aquino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquino,\_Italy "Aquino, Italy")_
*   The Scottish Christian scholar [Duns Scotus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duns_Scotus "Duns Scotus"), meaning _Duns the Scot_
*   The [Netherlandish artist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netherlandish_art "Netherlandish art")[Hieronymus Bosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieronymus_Bosch "Hieronymus Bosch"), originally the Dutch _Jheronimus_ plus his hometown of _[The Bosch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%27s-Hertogenbosch "'s-Hertogenbosch")_
*   The explorer [Columbus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Columbus "Christopher Columbus"), from his original [Western Romance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Romance "Western Romance") name of _Corombo_ or _Colombo_
*   The [Polish-Prussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Prussia "Royal Prussia") astronomer [Nicolaus Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus "Nicolaus Copernicus"), from the [Middle Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Low_German "Middle Low German")_Niclas_ or _Niklas_ plus _Koppernigk_ (lit.'from [Koperniki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koperniki "Koperniki")': his father's hometown)

In English, place names often appear in Latinised form. This is a result of many early text books mentioning the places being written in Latin. Because of this, the English language often uses Latinised forms of foreign place names instead of anglicised forms or the original names.

Examples of Latinised names for countries or regions are:

*   [Estonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonia "Estonia") (Estonian name _Eesti_, Dutch/German/Scandinavian name _Estland_, i.e. 'land of the [Aesti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesti "Aesti")')
*   [Ingria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingria "Ingria") (Finnish _Inkerinmaa_, German/Scandinavian _Ingermanland_, i.e. 'land of the [Ingermans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Izhorians "Izhorians")', the local tribe)
*   [Livonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livonia "Livonia") (German/Scandinavian name _Livland_', i.e. 'land of the [Livs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livonian_people "Livonian people")', the local tribe)

_[Eboracum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eboracum "Eboracum")_ was the Latinised name for the modern English city [York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/York "York"). It is a Latinised form of the [Brittonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Brittonic "Common Brittonic") name *_Eburākon_ which means 'place of (the) yew trees'. The Common Brittonic language was spoken by the [indigenous people of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celtic_Britons "Celtic Britons")[and evolved into](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brythonic_Languages "Brythonic Languages") modern [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language "Welsh language"), [Cornish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornish_language_revival "Cornish language revival"), and [Breton (Brezhoneg)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breton_language "Breton language").

Latinisation is a common practice for [scientific names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_name "Scientific name"). For example, _[Livistona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livistona "Livistona")_, the name of a genus of palm trees, is a Latinisation of _[Livingstone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livingstone\_(name) "Livingstone (name)")_.

## Historical background

[[edit](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Latinisation_of_names&action=edit&section=5 "Edit section: Historical background")]

During the age of the [Roman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire "Roman Empire"), translation of names into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") (in the West) or [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks "Greeks") (in the East) was common. Additionally, Latinised versions of Greek [substantives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substantive "Substantive"), particularly [proper nouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_noun "Proper noun"), could easily be [declined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Declension "Declension") by Latin speakers with minimal modification of the original word.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names#cite_note-4)

During the [medieval period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_period "Medieval period"), after the Empire collapsed in [Western Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Europe "Western Europe"), the main bastion of scholarship was the [Roman Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic_Church "Roman Catholic Church"), for which Latin was the primary written language. In the early medieval period, most European scholars were priests and most educated people spoke Latin, and as a result, Latin became firmly established as the scholarly language for the West.

By the early 19th century, Europe had largely abandoned Latin as a scholarly language (most scientific studies and scholarly publications are printed in English), but a variety of fields still use Latin terminology as the norm. By tradition, it is still common in some fields to name new discoveries in Latin. And because Western science became dominant during the 18th and 19th centuries, the use of Latin names in many scholarly fields has gained worldwide acceptance, at least when European languages are being used for communication.
